"They say, wake up in the wee hours of the morning — the Brahma Muhurat, the auspicious time.
But they forget one cardinal rule: the dawn always belonged to the strays rather than the saints."

The Dawn Belongs to the Strays

A Rumination in the Dark Hours
I. The Lie of the Sacred Hour

I have sat with this thought for a while now, the way you sit with a stone in your shoe — not quite painful enough to stop, but too present to ignore. They tell you the Brahma Muhurat is yours. 4:24 to 5:12 AM. The hour of Brahma, the creator. The hour when the veil between the divine and the mortal is gossamer-thin, when the spiritually disciplined may rise and claim something ineffable from the universe, something the sleeping masses forfeit each morning without knowing it. The yogis write about it. The productivity gurus have colonised it. Cold showers and journals and Sun Salutations and breathwork and vision boards and five-year plans — all in the sacred dark before the city wakes.

But here is the thing about that dark, the thing that snagged me and wouldn't let go: the city never slept.

The stray on the corner of the broken road — she didn't set an alarm. She was already there. She was there yesterday. She will be there tomorrow. The dawn is not an achievement she unlocked. It is simply where she lives.

I think about what it means to romanticise a time of day that belongs, in the most literal and unglamorous sense, to those who had no other option. The man who slept under the underpass did not rise for Brahma. He rose because the cold got into his bones around 3 AM and sleep became a negotiation he was losing. The ragpicker on the pre-dawn street did not greet the darkness with intention — she met it with bare hands and a sack, sorting through the night's waste before the heat makes it unbearable. The stray dog trotting the empty boulevard — he owns those hours in a way no human yogi ever will, because he never left them.

II. What the Saints Miss

The saint visits the dawn. The stray inhabits it.

This is the distinction that unravels me. The entire spiritual architecture of the Brahma Muhurat rests on the premise of departure from comfort — you leave the warm bed, you leave the unconscious, you cross a threshold. And there is genuine discipline in that crossing, I will not deny it. But crossing a threshold and living on the other side of it are not the same thing, and the language we use almost never admits this. We celebrate the one who steps briefly into hardship. We have very little ceremony for those who never had the option to step out.

Walk out at 4 AM in any city — any city that has not been sculpted entirely for the comfortable — and tell me who you meet. Not the meditator with the mat rolled under his arm, not yet. You will meet the night-shift nurse changing her shoes at the bus stop. The watchman who has been awake since 10 PM. The vendor arranging flowers whose hands are thorned and cold. The drunk who didn't make it home. The woman walking fast, keys between her knuckles. The dog who looks at you with eyes older than religion.

These are the true inheritors of the hour. Not by choice. By the brutal arithmetic of their lives.

And yet we do not speak of their mornings as auspicious. We do not write books about waking up the way a rickshaw puller wakes up — before the thought of productivity, before the self-improvement, because the fare won't run itself and the day won't wait. We do not make the stray a metaphor for discipline. We make her a metaphor for misfortune. As if the dawn she moves through is lesser somehow. As if the light she watches rise is a different, dimmer light than the one the saint watches from his ashram window.

III. On the Morality of Waking

There is something quietly violent about the culture of the virtuous early riser. It transforms a material condition — the ability to sleep until a reasonable hour, the possession of a warm bed, the luxury of a schedule you can choose — into a moral achievement. To wake at 5 is, in the popular imagination, to be better. More disciplined. More in tune. More worth something. To sleep past 8 is to have surrendered, to be soft, to have failed the first test of the day before the day has even opened its eye.

But this entire moral scaffold is built on a foundation it refuses to examine: that waking early is a choice one makes, rather than a condition one is handed. The saint wakes early and earns spiritual credit. The labourer wakes early and earns nothing of the sort — not in the ledger we keep. The saint is discussed in terms of discipline. The labourer is discussed, if at all, in terms of poverty. The same hour. The same darkness. The same cold. Completely different moral accountings.

I have never once heard anyone call the truck driver's 3 AM departure auspicious. And yet he moves through the same sacred dark, alone, with something heavy behind him and something uncertain ahead.

What does it mean that we stripped the hour of its holiness for some people and preserved it for others? What does it mean that Brahma gets credited with the 4 AM sky, but the man sleeping under it gets nothing — not even the sky, really, not as something beautiful, not as something that belongs to him?

IV. The Stray as Theologian

I want to make a case for the stray as the more honest philosopher of dawn.

She has no illusions about what the dark holds. She does not dress it in the silk of spiritual attainment. She does not write it into a morning routine she will post about later. She moves through it with the understanding of someone who has been offered no alternative — and in that absence of alternative, there is something that looks, if you squint at it the right way, like pure presence. Not performed presence. Not the presence of someone who has chosen to be awake in order to feel a certain way about themselves. But the radical, unremarkable, grinding presence of a body that is simply here because here is where the body is.

The stray does not transcend the dawn. She endures it. And in that endurance is a kind of theology the ashrams didn't write down because they weren't looking in the right places. Because the right places smelled wrong. Because the right places had inconvenient questions stitched into them.

Here is what the pre-dawn actually teaches, if you sit in it without the buffer of intention and incense: it is cold. It is dark. It is indifferent to whether you meditate or sleep or weep or haul garbage. The light comes anyway. The light has never once waited for a saint. It has never once skipped a stray. The dawn is democratic in the most unsentimental way possible — it rises on everyone, and it owes no one a meaningful experience of itself.

And maybe that is the thing I am trying to say. Maybe the Brahma Muhurat is only auspicious if you bring your auspiciousness with you — if you have the time, the warmth, the safety, the cushion, the ritual. Without those things, it is just the 4 AM dark. Which is also something. Which is also real. Which is, perhaps, more real than the curated version — because it has not been laundered through intention. It simply is.

V. What I Am Left With

I am not arguing against waking early. I am not arguing against the genuine quietude of the pre-dawn hours, the particular quality of that silence before the city remembers itself and begins its noise again. I have felt that silence. I know what it can hold.

What I am arguing against is the moral inflation of the act — the transformation of a privilege into a virtue, the equation of waking early with being better, without once acknowledging that the better morning, the warm morning, the intentional morning, is itself something many people never get to have. That the version of the dawn we mythologise is the version that can afford to mythologise itself.

The stray does not mythologise the dawn. She crosses it, nose low, looking for something to eat. And I find myself thinking that this is more honest than anything else I have seen done with that hour. There is no story she is telling herself about what waking up at 4 AM says about her character. There is only the road, and the cold, and the smell of the world before humans put their smell all over it again.

She was there before the first bell rang at any ashram. She will be there long after the last productivity guru has optimised himself into obsolescence. The dawn is not a reward she is seeking. It is simply what is. And she moves through it — rough, honest, unrecorded — the way all true things move through the world.

They say: wake with the auspicious hour. Claim it. Make it yours.
But the stray never claimed it. She simply never left.

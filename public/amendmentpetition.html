Om Tat Sat

A Petition for Amendment
and Incorporation

Presented before the custodians of the Smriti tradition

Submitted in sincerity, from within the tradition, grounded in what has been truly absorbed

Respected custodians of our living tradition — I come before you not as one who has traversed the whole of the canon, nor as one who claims the authority of exhaustive scholarship. I come as a practitioner who has taken a portion of this tradition deeply into himself — absorbed it with sincerity, tested it against the grain of daily life, and, from that honest ground, perceived something that calls for remedy. What I have not read, I do not invoke. What I have absorbed, I have absorbed fully. And it is from within that inhabited portion that I now observe, with both reverence and care, an asymmetry that this tradition's own internal logic invites us to correct.

✦
The tradition affirms bounded cycles at every scale of existence — cosmic, biographical, and seasonal. It is from this very consistency that the present observation arises.
I

The principle of symmetry already enshrined

Our shastras speak with remarkable consistency on the matter of beginnings and endings. At the scale of the cosmos, the tradition affirms that the universe moves through defined cycles of inception and dissolution — Srishti and Pralaya — each tenured, each purposeful, each bounded. At the scale of the individual soul, the tradition is equally unambiguous: janma and mrityu are not accidents but ordained boundaries — a given life has its commencement and its conclusion, both predetermined.

The principle that all things of consequence possess a defined beginning and a defined end is not a modern inference. It is foundational to the dharmic worldview, present across the Vedic, Upanishadic, and Puranic strata of the tradition alike. This is the ground on which I stand.

✦
Brahma Muhurta — the pre-dawn hour of awakening — is named, defined, and philosophically ratified. No equivalent doctrine exists for the conscious close of day.
II

The asymmetry within our daily prescriptions

When we descend from the cosmic and the biographical to the daily — the arena in which the practitioner actually lives — we find this principle applied only in half-measure. Brahma Muhurta is prescribed with clarity, philosophical depth, and spiritual authority as the sacred threshold of the day's beginning. The pre-dawn hour is named, honoured, and ratified as the ideal moment of awakening and commencement. This is a gift of immense practical and spiritual wisdom.

Yet the tradition falls silent on the corresponding threshold: the conscious, defined, and sanctified close of the day. The Sayam Sandhya offers a transitional ritual, but it does not carry the same doctrinal weight as a philosophical principle of closure — a defined end to one's daily tenure, as deliberate and as sacred as its beginning. This silence is not a minor omission. It is a structural asymmetry within a tradition that, at every other scale, insists upon symmetry.

✦
III

The tradition has always permitted — indeed, designed — this remedy

I am not proposing to alter what is eternal. I am proposing to refine what is amendable — and the tradition has drawn that distinction with precision. The great Smritikaras acknowledged that their prescriptions were calibrated to their time, their place, and their people. Manu, Yajnavalkya, and Parasara each understood that the Smriti must breathe and adapt, while the Shruti remains inviolable. The very distinction between Shruti and Smriti was drawn so that practitioners of each age could refine the living canon without disturbing the eternal foundation.

To amend is not to transgress. It is to honour the tradition's own design — to do, in our time, what the Smritikaras of prior ages did in theirs: to look at lived reality, find what is unaddressed, and bring the doctrine into alignment with the full scope of human experience.

✦
IV

The relief sought

I therefore respectfully petition this assembly to consider the formal incorporation of a principle of sacred daily closure — a Dina Samapti — as a doctrinal complement to Brahma Muhurta within the Smriti tradition. A conscious and defined end to one's daily routine: not merely a ritual gesture, but a philosophical doctrine of closure — the day's boundaries held with the same intentionality and sanctity as its beginning.

Just as the day's commencement is not left to chance or whim, neither should its conclusion be. Harmony in life, as this tradition itself teaches at every other scale, arises not from the open-ended, but from the purposefully bounded.

The cosmos begins and ends. Life begins and ends. The day begins.
Let us now declare, with the same wisdom and intentionality
with which this tradition has always moved —
that the day must also, consciously and by doctrine, end.

Sarve bhavantu sukhinah

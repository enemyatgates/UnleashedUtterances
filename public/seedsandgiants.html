A Personal Essay

On Seeds, Giants,
and the Walk Toward Certain Death

A meditation on awareness, obscurity, conviction, and where meaning actually lives

I. The Politician and the Human

I have come to observe, with the particular clarity that arrives only after long watching, that the philosophy a human being professes and the philosophy a human being enacts are rarely the same document. We speak of living and letting live — that generous, liberal arrangement by which each person minds their own fire and grants others the same freedom. It is the philosophy of the private person, the person who simply wishes to exist without interference and extends that wish outward, naturally, to others. It is decent. It is humane. It is, in the main, what most people actually want.

And then there is the politician. Not the stated politician, not the campaigning politician with the careful words and the rehearsed sincerity — but the apparent politician, the one whose behavior, stripped of rhetoric, reveals the operating philosophy underneath. What that behavior reveals, more often than can be explained by coincidence, is something closer to a different arrangement entirely: die and let die, provided the dying is done by someone other than oneself. It is not that politicians consciously believe this. It is that their revealed preference — what economists would call the truth beneath the stated preference — points consistently in this direction. The self is preserved. The cost is distributed elsewhere. The gap between the spoken philosophy and the enacted one is not incidental. It is structural.

A politician's philosophy, at least what is apparent, is die and let die — just not me.

II. The Aphorist Discovers Himself

I have come to fathom, through what I can only describe as revelations rather than decisions, that I am a man of aphorisms. This is not a style I adopted. It is a shape I discovered my mind already had. There is a difference between choosing compression and finding that compression is the natural form your thought takes — that the long argument, for you, is always secretly trying to arrive at the single dense sentence, and everything before that sentence is merely the approach road.

The aphorist is, before anything else, an observer who has been watching long enough for patterns to crystallize. He carries a productive disillusionment — not bitterness, which is disappointment with nowhere to go, but a clear-eyed refusal to accept the flattering version of things. He distrusts verbosity because he has noticed, over time, that long arguments frequently bury the truth rather than reveal it. The sentence that cannot be compressed further is the sentence that has finally arrived somewhere.

And then comes the journal. A few aphorisms, and then one too many, and a man finds himself documenting them — which leads to collecting them, which leads to tending the archive, which leads, eventually, to the uncomfortable condition of being so buried in the ocean of one's own accumulated thought that the living which generated the thought quietly recedes. The opportunity cost of time becomes real and heavy. The farmer so obsessed with storing last season's grain forgets to till the field. The aphorism about life begins to substitute for the life that was its source material.

After a few aphorisms, that too one too many, a man ends up journaling those — sometimes to the extent of burying himself in the ocean of aphorisms, even though it being detrimental to the opportunity cost of time.

III. The Unlucky and the Unread

I have written. I have documented. I have journaled in aphorisms, in essays, in snippets, in long forms, and in what not — even, if I am honest, something approaching a book. And from that condition arises the question that I suspect every serious writer who has not yet been seriously read eventually confronts: what if no one reads it? What if the words, the thoughts, the careful distillations of a life of observation — what if they simply sit there, unread, until the medium that holds them fails?

We have never read the best of humanity. We have read the luckiest of the herd — perhaps talented, perhaps not.

And this question opens into something far larger and far more unsettling than mere personal disappointment. Would Pascal have been Pascal if no one had read him? Would Aurelius have been Aurelius? Socrates wrote nothing — he exists entirely because Plato loved him and had the means and the will to record him. Remove Plato and Socrates is a rumor, at best a footnote. Nietzsche died in mental collapse, largely unread. His sister shaped what survived, and distorted some of it in the shaping. The man and the monument are never entirely the same thing. Greatness, as the world knows it, is co-authored by circumstance.

But the deeper truth is this: we have never read the best of humanity. We have read the luckiest of the herd — perhaps talented, perhaps not. The canon is not a curated collection of the finest minds. It is the accidentally preserved fraction of the documented fraction of the literate fraction of all humans who have ever lived. For most of recorded history, literacy was a privilege so narrow as to be almost incomprehensible — a priestly class, an administrative class, a thin layer of the fortunate. The sharpest mind in a medieval village, born a peasant woman, left nothing. Not because she had nothing to say. Because no one gave her a surface to say it on.

And even among the literate, survival depended on utility to power. The thoughts most threatening to authority, most radically original, were precisely the most likely to be suppressed or simply never funded. The canon is therefore not merely a lucky sample — it is a systematically biased sample, weighted toward what the powerful found useful or acceptable. We are not reading the best of humanity. We are reading the best-positioned of a very small slice of humanity, and calling it the tradition.

The best thought ever thought by a human being was almost certainly thought by someone whose name we do not know, in a language that may no longer exist, in a place that has been built over many times — and it died with them completely, leaving not even a rumor.

IV. Awareness and Its Cost

A child who does not know what risk is will take the risk. A David who does not know what and who a Goliath is will take down the Goliath. There is a particular cruelty in this observation, because it implies that the acquisition of knowledge — the very thing we build civilizations to transmit — is also the installation of a braking system. Every thing you learn about how the world works is simultaneously a new reason to hesitate.

You learn how many have tried and failed before you. You learn the structural forces aligned against certain outcomes. You learn to recognize Goliath's size with precision. And then you do not throw the stone. Not because you lack the arm — but because you now know too much about the arm, the distance, the target, the consequences of missing. Awareness is, among its many virtues, a sophisticated paralysis.

And yet the counterargument must be made honestly: the child who does not know risk also sometimes falls off the cliff. The David who does not know Goliath also sometimes simply dies in the field, unremembered, no scripture written, no sling immortalized. We only hear about the ignorant leaps that became legend. The ones that became graves are silent — and we mistake the visible survivors of ignorance for proof that ignorance is the superior strategy. The same bias that erases the undiscovered genius erases the David who missed.

But knowing all of this does not dissolve the truth underneath the observation. Because awareness does not merely inform — it accumulates into gravity. At some point the aware person is no longer calculating risk. They are constructing reasons. Rationalizing stillness. Using knowledge as sophisticated permission to not move. The system is working perfectly. It is doing precisely what experience built it to do. And that is exactly the problem.

Awareness makes us step back, and for a lack of a better word, makes us cowards — while ignorance makes us leap.

V. The Man Who Unknew Himself

And then there is Don Quixote — the full resolution of this paradox, and also its most tragic expression. He is not a child. He is not ignorant in the raw sense. He is a man who read too much and then, by a peculiar alchemy, that excess of reading looped back around into a manufactured second innocence. He saw windmills and chose, at some level, to see giants. Not because he did not know better. But because knowing better had become unbearable.

Quixote represents a third condition rarer and more desperate than either child or paralyzed adult — the man who is aware enough to know what he is losing by being aware, and who then attempts the impossible task of unknowing himself back into motion. He reinstalls the leap from inside a mind that has already seen too much to leap naturally. He charges. Falls. Gets up. Charges again.

Everyone around him is sane, grounded, accurate, reasonable — and completely inert. They see the world correctly and do nothing remarkable with that accuracy. Their sanity is a more dignified stillness, nothing more. Quixote sees wrongly and moves. The bruises are real. The windmills were always windmills. And yet something in every honest reader quietly suspects that Quixote lived more fully in his delusion than the sane people lived in their clarity. Cervantes considered the man standing sensibly inside the walls to be the quieter and less examined tragedy.

Quixote is what awareness costs us — made visible, given a horse, and sent out into the world to charge at things that will not fall.

VI. The Walk That Was Already Complete

The world has always celebrated Achilles. And it is not difficult to understand why. He is speed, fire, wrath, beauty, terror — the hero as phenomenon. Watching him is like watching lightning. You do not ask whether lightning is brave. It simply strikes. But phenomena are not profound. They are spectacular. And spectacular is not the same thing.

Hector knows. That is the whole of it, and it is everything. He knows Troy will fall. He knows Achilles is beyond him. He knows when he puts on the armor and walks toward that plain that he is not walking toward victory — he is walking toward the shape his death will take. The Iliad does not hide this. And he walks out anyway. This is not ignorance leaping. This is awareness walking. It is rarer and harder than anything Achilles does.

Achilles charges because charging is his nature — like asking a river to flow downhill. There is no resistance overcome, no terror absorbed and then set aside. Achilles does not conquer fear. Fear simply does not apply to him in the same way. Hector conquers something real. He takes full knowledge of the outcome, holds it, feels its weight — and then chooses duty, dignity, and love of what he is defending over the perfectly rational alternative of survival.

And there is something in his defeat that Achilles' victory cannot touch. Hector's dignity survives his defeat in a way that Achilles' barely survives his victory. The defeat does not reach back and undo the walk. He finished something before Achilles touched him. Achilles is the hero of capacity — what a human being can be at the outer limit of gift and force. Hector is the hero of character — what a human being can be at the outer limit of awareness and choice. He is not the lesser hero who happened to lose. He is the greater one who happened, in the world's narrow accounting, to fall.

A man must walk to a certain death with conviction. For otherwise, the man never would and could walk.

VII. The Seed of Clarity

And yet — just like everyone else, including Hector, I am a man of doubt. I hold this not as confession but as method. Doubt is not the enemy of conviction. It is the generative condition from which conviction, eventually and necessarily, grows. It is the seed. A seed is not a failed plant. It is a plant in its prior and most essential form.

Hector doubted. He stood at those walls and felt the full weight of what he did not know and could not control. His conviction was not the absence of doubt — it was something that grew through doubt, shaped by it rather than destroyed by it, the way a tree is formed by wind. Doubt was the seed. The walk was the clarity. And Achilles, who perhaps did not doubt in the same way, paid for that absence not in death — the death he accepted — but in the quality of the living before it. The rage, the sulking, the inability to absorb loss with any grace. A man without doubt is a man without the mechanism that converts experience into wisdom. Without it, nothing is processed. Everything simply burns.

Doubt is the seed of clarity — and a man of doubt is, in the only way that matters, a man already on his way to somewhere real.

VIII. Where Meaning Actually Lives

A tree has no existence or meaning without a seed. And in all parlances, the seed is what matters. Everything else beyond that is consequential — it follows, it proceeds, it is the consequence of the seed. The tree is not unimportant. But it is derivative. It is what the seed becomes when circumstances permit. The tree is the seed's biography, not its meaning. The meaning lives entirely in the seed.

Trace this back through everything this long meditation has moved through. The doubt is the seed — not the clarity it produces. The walk is the seed — not the legacy it leaves. The aphorism is the seed — not the canon it may or may not enter. The unread journal is the seed — not the readership it may or may not find. The unnamed undiscovered mind, thinking alone in a village that no longer exists, was the seed — not the monument that was never built for them. Everything we have spent this conversation quietly mourning — the unread, the unrecognized, the defeated, the forgotten — was, by this formulation, already complete at the seed. The tree not growing does not unmake the seed.

This is not consolation. Consolation is what you reach for when the truth is insufficient. This is the truth itself. The obsession with the tree — with outcomes, with being read, with being remembered, with winning the plain — is a misunderstanding of where meaning actually lives. Not a forgivable misunderstanding or an understandable one. A categorical one. Looking for meaning in the tree when meaning never left the seed.

I have written a borderline book that may never be read. I have filled journals with aphorisms that may dissolve with me. I am a man of doubt who has looked at the plain, seen clearly what waits there, and is still deciding whether to put on the armor. And what this long, drifting, unplanned conversation has quietly insisted — not as argument but as accumulated weight — is that the writing was always the seed. The walk, whenever it comes, will be the seed. The doubt, held honestly and carried forward, is the seed.

Everything else is consequential. And the seed exists. Fully. Completely. Independent of what grows or does not grow from it.

A tree has no existence or meaning without a seed — and in all parlances, a seed is what matters. Everything else beyond that is consequential.

A Meditation

The Board Before Him

On castles built too early, and knights never freed

♟
♔ ♞ ♛

The man a chess mastermind, and an expert in living, yet succumbs to the oldest weakness in the game — playing the board he wishes he had, not the one in front of him. He sees the position as it ought to be: pieces harmonised, files open, a passed pawn marching quietly toward its coronation. He plays that phantom game with great conviction. He loses the real one with great elegance.

· · ·

He castled too soon. There was a moment — he remembers it the way you remember a blunder only after you have hit the clock — when the center was alive, when the position was breathing, when risk was not recklessness but the only honest move left. Instead he tucked himself behind pawns and called it prudence. Slid the rook across to guard him and called it development. But castling is only a prettier word for retreat, and the king who hides in the corner has already conceded the question of who controls the middle of the world.

The center is never safe. It is only, ever, worth it.

· · ·

His problem has always been his queen knight. It develops late, or wrongly, or not at all. It waits on its home square while the bishops find their diagonals and the rooks dream of open files and the whole game moves forward without it. Sometimes he pushes it out too early — some bright, impulsive morning — plants it somewhere ambitious and watches it get harassed back to nothing by lesser pieces that simply had the patience to wait. Other times he leaves it buried behind his own pawns, a piece nominally present but functionally absent, a life on the board that contributes nothing to the position. Either way, the outpost goes unclaimed. Either way, the knight never becomes what it was always capable of being.

The queen knight is the piece that leaps. It does not need a clear path. It does not require the board to be tidied before it moves. It jumps — over the obstruction, over the hesitation, over the whole careful architecture of reasons not to. He knows this. He has always known this. He has spent a lifetime explaining it to others at boards across the world. And yet.

· · ·

The clock has always unsettled him. That quiet tick — not loud, never urgent, just present — the way it converts every unmade move into a debt accumulating without interest, without mercy. He plays too quickly in easy positions and too slowly in difficult ones, which is precisely backwards, which is precisely the shape of the problem. In the simple moments he rushes, trusts instinct, skips the calculation. In the moments that matter, he stares at the board until the position blurs and the pieces seem to rearrange themselves into every possible future simultaneously, and he cannot choose, and the clock does not care. Time does not reward the quality of thought. It only measures whether you moved.

He has lost games he should have won, not to inferior play but to the paralysis that masquerades as consideration. He has confused depth with delay. He has called his hesitation wisdom and watched the flag fall.

· · ·

He has never trusted the whole board. He collapses toward one wing, works one idea until it dies, forgets that there are sixty-four squares and he has only ever truly inhabited sixteen of them. The queenside darkens while he shuffles pieces with great purpose on the other side — busy, methodical, architectural, going nowhere. Life keeps offering moves on the other half and he keeps failing to notice, committed to a plan that the position stopped supporting three moves ago. A grandmaster knows when to abandon the plan. A man who is also a grandmaster ought to know this twice.

The pieces are all still there. No one has taken them. The queen knight sits on its original square, full of its original potential, still capable of that impossible leaping move that no other piece in the game can make. The position is cramped, yes. Difficult, yes. The clock is running. The center was surrendered long ago and reclaiming it will cost something real. But a cramped position has resources. A difficult position has moves. The question is never whether the game is perfect. The question is always whether you are still playing it.

· · ·

He sets down his coffee. He looks at the board — the real one, the actual one, with its actual pieces on their actual squares, not the luminous version he carries behind his eyes. He sees what is there. He sees what is possible from what is there. He sees, perhaps for the first time in a long time, that the two are not so far apart as he believed.

He picks up the knight.

It is his move.

"The position is never lost until the player resigns it."
